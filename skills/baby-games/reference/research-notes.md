# Research notes behind the baby-games skill

Gathered November 2026. Numbers in [brackets] refer to `sources.md`. Where the evidence
is thin (much of children's interface design is practitioner guidance, not trials),
that is said.

## 1. What each age can do

CDC "Learn the Signs. Act Early." milestones (2022 revision; "most children (75%+) can
do by this age") [1]:

- **18 months**: points to show interest; tries 3+ words; follows one-step directions
  without gestures; scribbles; looks at a few pages of a book with you.
- **2 years**: points to pictures in a book when asked ("Where is the bear?"); 2-word
  phrases; points to 2 body parts; tries switches, knobs and buttons on a toy.
- **30 months**: about 50 words; names things in a book when you point; follows
  two-step instructions; **shows he knows at least one colour** (points to red when asked).
- **3 years**: conversation with 2+ exchanges; asks who/what/where/why; names the
  action in a picture; draws a circle when shown; threads big beads.
- **4 years**: **names a few colours**; tells what comes next in a known story; holds a
  crayon between fingers and thumb.
- **5 years**: **counts to 10; names some numbers 1–5 when pointed to; names some
  letters when pointed to; writes some letters of their name**; recognises simple
  rhymes; **pays attention for 5–10 minutes** in activities like story time
  (CDC adds: screen time doesn't count).

Implications: under 2, naming pictures and cause-and-effect; colours by pointing at
2½, naming at 4; letter naming is a 5-year milestone, so letters for 3–4-year-olds are
exposure and recognition, not drills. Attention of 5–10 min at 5 sets session lengths.

**Colours and shapes** [12]: comprehension of basic colour words is usually reported
around 30 months; producing colour words keeps developing to 4–5. Recognising circle
and square around 3, naming circle/square/triangle by about 4–4½; matching and sorting
shapes in play earlier, often by 2. Recognition precedes naming → ask "where is…",
not "what is…".

**Number** [9]: perceptual subitising (seeing how many without counting) — many children
subitise up to 3 by age 2, up to 5 at 4–5; conceptual subitising (seeing 5 as 3 + 2)
grows to 10 by about 6–7. Clements & Sarama's learning trajectories (and the IES
"Teaching Math to Young Children" practice guide module) build counting from small,
patterned sets. → subitise 1–3 first, then count to 5, then 10.

**Emotions** [15]: children match emotion faces before they can name them; happy and
sad (or angry) are usually the first two emotion words, around 3½; fear and surprise
around 4½–5. Explicit labels help learning. → feelings games from 3, happy/sad first,
match faces before naming.

**Frameworks for the skills taxonomy**:
- Head Start ELOF (birth to 5) [3]: five central domains — Approaches to Learning;
  Social & Emotional Development; Language & Literacy; Cognition; Perceptual, Motor &
  Physical Development. Preschool literacy splits into Phonological Awareness, Print &
  Alphabet Knowledge, Comprehension & Text Structure, Writing; preschool cognition into
  Mathematics Development (Counting & Cardinality, Operations & Algebraic Thinking,
  Measurement, Geometry & Spatial Sense) and Scientific Reasoning; infant/toddler
  cognition includes Emergent Mathematical Thinking and Exploration & Discovery.
- EYFS (England, birth to 5) [4]: three prime areas (Communication & Language;
  Physical Development; Personal, Social & Emotional Development) and four specific
  areas (Literacy; Mathematics; Understanding the World; Expressive Arts & Design).

## 2. Screen time

- **AAP, "Media and Young Minds" (2016)** [2]: under 18 months avoid screen media other
  than video chat; 18–24 months, if introduced, high-quality content watched *with* a
  parent; 2–5 years about 1 hour a day of high-quality programming, co-viewed so adults
  help children understand and apply it; media should be a shared experience; avoid
  fast-paced programs and apps with a lot of distracting content (as summarised by
  AAP's parent pages); no screens during meals and for an hour before bed.
- **WHO (2019)** [5]: under 1, no screen time; at 1, sedentary screen time not
  recommended; 2–4, no more than 1 hour, less is better.
- **NAEYC & Fred Rogers Center position statement (2012)** [6]: technology is a tool
  when used intentionally and developmentally appropriately; discourage passive,
  non-interactive use; prefer active, hands-on, creative, and shared use with adults;
  consider public health limits.

How a game respects this: short fixed sessions; a natural ending (finish screen, no
auto-next); co-play friendly for the youngest (a grown-up can sit with the child —
nothing is too fast to talk about); Cru suggests breaks; no hooks to keep playing.

## 3. Learning and motivation

- **Four pillars** (Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015) [7]: children learn from apps that are
  *active* (minds-on, not just tapping), *engaged* (not distracted by bells and
  whistles), *meaningful* (connected to their life), and *socially interactive* (with
  a person — here Cru and a parent), in service of a clear learning goal.
- **Errorless learning** [13]: small studies with preschoolers (including p/q and b/d
  discrimination with stimulus fading, 1976) found fading and prompting procedures
  produced fewer errors and comparable or faster learning than trial and error, and
  most-to-least prompting produced fewer errors than least-to-most. → errorless for
  the youngest (answer highlighted, distractors weak), then fade help.
- **Scaffolding** (Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976 — the classic definition: an adult
  controls the parts of the task beyond the learner so the learner can do the rest):
  → hint, narrow choices, show; give the final step back to the child.
- **Praise** [8]: Gunderson et al. (2013) — the share of *process* praise (effort,
  actions, strategies) parents gave 1–3-year-olds predicted children's incremental
  ("abilities grow") mindset and preference for challenge at 7–8; a follow-up linked it
  to later maths and reading. Correlational, but consistent with Dweck's experiments.
  → praise what the child did, not who they are.
- **Difficulty**: aim for high success (~80% first-try) — the "desirable difficulty"
  for adults is too much for toddlers; motivation at this age comes from competence.
  Fast step-down prevents a run of failures; slow step-up prevents a sudden wall.

## 4. Designing for small hands and pre-readers

- **Targets** [10]: Nielsen Norman Group recommends touch targets of at least 2 × 2 cm
  for young children (vs 1 × 1 cm for adults); small, close targets caused accidental
  taps and, in one test, a 5 mm close button sent 7-year-olds to an app store. Under 5:
  avoid two-handed actions, quick responses to visual stimuli and combined gestures;
  avoid precise drags; offer drag *or* tap.
- **Toddler touch** [11]: in a CHI Play 2018 study of 42 children aged 18–42 months,
  common errors were unintended movement, pressing too hard (long-press by accident),
  and imprecision partly due to target size. Abdul Aziz et al.: 2-year-olds manage tap
  and drag with difficulties; 3-year-olds most gestures; 4-year-olds all tested gestures.
  UXPA: under 5, drag-and-drop often breaks as fingers lift; offer tap-then-tap.
- **Sesame Workshop / Joan Ganz Cooney Center, "Best Practices: Designing Touch Tablet
  Experiences for Preschoolers" (2012)** [14]: tapping is easiest; pinch and flick are
  hard; preschoolers rest wrists on the bottom edge, so keep hotspots away from it;
  pair dialogue with visual cues for non-readers; design for joint parent–child use.
- **Google "Designing for kids"** [16]: most children under 5 can't read; never rely on
  text or audio alone; feedback after every action with sound and visuals; for wrong
  answers use visual hints (arrows, highlights, pulses) toward the right answer, keep it
  positive and let them get back in quickly; avoid multi-finger gestures, double-tap,
  pinch; icons over text; adult-only areas behind a parental gate.
- **Cursor**: there is little published research on cursor size for children; NN/g
  notes children under 9 struggle with small objects and precise pointing on desktop
  [10]. A large, high-contrast cursor is a reasonable accommodation (operating systems
  offer the same for low vision). Browsers accept CSS cursor images up to 128 px;
  Chromium may fall back to the default near the window edge for cursors over 32 px.

## 5. Colour and sound

- **Contrast**: WCAG 2.x — 4.5:1 for text (7:1 enhanced), 3:1 for graphical objects
  needed to understand content (SC 1.4.11). Children's vision and attention favour
  bold, high-contrast, uncluttered images; the youngest track high contrast best.
- **Colour blindness**: about 8% of boys and 0.5% of girls of northern European
  descent have a red–green deficiency; never use colour as the only cue (WCAG 1.4.1).
  The Okabe-Ito palette [17] was designed to stay distinguishable for all common
  deficiencies; the skill's sets were checked with the Machado et al. (2009)
  simulation matrices and CIELAB ΔE (script result in SKILL.md).
- **Sound**: no harsh "wrong" buzzers (Google: keep error feedback positive [16]); soft,
  short tones; children's hearing is more sensitive to loud sudden sounds and WHO's
  safe-listening guidance for children is lower than for adults — keep game audio
  moderate and let the device volume, not the game, be the loud end.

## 6. Safety and ethics

- **Manipulative design** [18]: Radesky et al., JAMA Network Open 2022 — in apps played
  by 3–5-year-olds, only about 20% had no manipulative design; tactics included
  parasocial pressure (a character crying or begging when the child stops), fabricated
  time pressure (countdowns), navigation constraints, lures (shiny rewards to keep
  playing or buy) and ad pressure; lower-income children met more of it. → no crying
  characters, no countdowns, no streaks, no lures, no ads.
- **Store rules for kids' apps** [19]: Apple's Kids Category bans third-party ads and
  analytics in kids' apps, and requires a parental gate before links out or purchases;
  no personal data to third parties. → none of these exist in our games at all.
- **Photosensitive seizures** [20]: WCAG 2.3.1 — nothing flashes more than 3 times in
  any 1-second period unless below the general and red flash thresholds (a flash is a
  pair of opposing luminance changes of 10%+; any transition to or from saturated red
  is a red flash). → no strobing, no full-screen flashes, no red flashes; honour
  `prefers-reduced-motion` (WCAG 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions).

## 7. Letters and phonics

- **Order** [21]: many synthetic phonics programmes start with s, a, t, p, i, n (Letters
  and Sounds; Jolly Phonics s a t i p n), others differ (UFLI s m c t g p a o); no one
  order is proven best — start with common, easy-to-hear sounds, not A–Z.
- **Pure sounds** [21]: teachers "clip" the extra vowel off stop sounds (/b/ not "buh");
  continuous sounds (s, m, f) can be stretched.
- **b/d/p/q** [22]: reversals are developmentally normal until about 7; phonics
  programmes space out the introduction of b and d; a fading study taught p/q then b/d
  to preschoolers almost errorlessly [13]. → never test them against each other early.

## 8. How established makers do it (published material is limited)

- PBS KIDS publishes a brand style guide (contrast-checked colour pairs, type sizes)
  and playtests every game with children; its research-backed games pair a learning
  goal with a familiar character and audio instructions.
- Khan Academy Kids: free, no ads, no in-app purchases; an adaptive path that adjusts
  to each child; built with Stanford early-learning advisers; bold colours and
  hand-drawn warmth.
- Sesame Workshop: the 2012 touch-tablet best practices above; co-play ("joint media
  engagement") as the default use case.
- Toca Boca (open-ended play, no wrong answers, no text, no rules) shows the value of
  free exploration for the youngest — our 12–24-month band follows that model.

## 9. Trivia and facts for young children

There is little research specific to "trivia" at this age; it falls under ELOF
Scientific Reasoning / infant-toddler Exploration & Discovery and EYFS Understanding
the World. Children's "why" questions peak at 3–4 (CDC 3-year milestone: asks
who/what/where/why), so short true explanations meet a real appetite. Recognition
before recall applies again: picture choices, not open questions; always reveal the
answer, since the point is the fact, not the test.
